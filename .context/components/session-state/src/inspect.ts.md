The inspection tools report recorded facts and counts with references and never a verdict, score, or recommendation; the caller (a manager, usually) interprets. They read the database read-only and store nothing.
Why: the operator's direction (2026-10-26, `.ledger/202610242320-org-mri-morphogenesis/direction.md`) puts observation in the harness and judgment in the agents; a heuristic here would become a hidden optimizer.
Expertise comes from the runtime's index of `read`, `edit`, and `write` calls, counted whether or not they succeeded (the index records calls, not results); a compaction is reported as started, because the bridge records its start and a failed one looks the same: the indexed preview is the path argument as typed, resolved against the session's directory, and cut at 120 characters, so long paths still match by directory but not always by file. People and status come from `organizationIn` in the directory, so the tools and `directory` agree. A task's outcome is read straight from `assignments.outcome`, which only `finish` writes (unassigning leaves it null).
Why: the outcome needs no end-reason check as long as `work.ts` keeps outcome for finished assignments alone.
`expertsOn` and `areasOf` scope by the present roster (`subtreeOf`), because they answer who knows what now; their window only filters which calls count. `waitsIn` judges everything as of a moment instead: holders, managers, and subtree membership at the window's end for an open wait and at the resolution for a resolved one, from the fitness replay's `managerAt`.
Task-origin waits use `taskOwnership` from `fitness.ts`: structural ownership at the window's end for open waits and at resolution for unblocked waits. Recorder-based admission retains active-person membership at that moment. A retired organizational anchor can still own outstanding work.
Why: using today's task anchors made a later merge import already-resolved waits into an earlier organization's window; using active-person membership for task ownership would instead erase obligations on retirement.
`waitsIn` takes its open waits from `openWaits` in `fitness.ts`, so membership is judged as in fitness (historically, at the window's end). Inspection covers retained task-origin waits without fitness's temporary-peer-only task eligibility filter; neither view derives snapshot candidates from priced work.
Why: a pending link can outlive its source task's economic relevance. Inspection asks what is waiting, while fitness retains its established exclusion of program-internal task contributions. Two people are on one team when one manages the other or they share a non-null manager. A wait link points from the work or person waiting (its source) to what it needs (its target). The waiters are the source task's holders at the judged moment, or its source person; the recorder is the fallback when neither supplies a waiter. Target owners are the target person or the target task's holders at that moment. A wait crosses teams only when it has at least one target owner and no waiter shares a team with any of those owners.
`workIn` classifies each retained open task once: an explicit source-task wait takes precedence over assignment state; otherwise a held task is in progress and an unheld task is ready. Snapshot scope uses holders and structural ownership at the cutoff; arrivals and closes use their event times. Close-event scope includes assignments ending exactly at the close so finishing work does not remove it from the finishing person's results. Snapshot assignments retain their half-open intervals. Task metadata and link references suffice; no goal files or new state are needed.
Why: assignment state describes who holds work, not whether a model is currently running. Close-event scoping must still show a finished task to the person whose assignment ended with that close; applying snapshot-exclusive assignment ends to that event would hide the completion.
